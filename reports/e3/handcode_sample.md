## story 1

On the fourth night, Julian stopped pretending to sleep.

The hospital hummed around him: the IV pump, the distant elevator, the wet rattle of his own lungs. Pneumonia, they'd said, as if it were a small word. Without his suit, his phone full of clients, and his title, he was just a man in a gown with a wristband that spelled his name wrong.

Marisol came in at two, as she had every night, moving quietly in her soft-soled shoes. She checked his oxygen, adjusted the pillow he hadn't asked her to adjust.

"You're awake," she said.

"Apparently I'm too tired to sleep."

She laughed under her breath. "That's the worst kind."

He expected her to leave. Instead she sat on the edge of the visitor's chair, as though she had a minute to spare, which he doubted. "My husband didn't speak to his brother for eleven years," she said. "Over a truck, if you can believe it. Their father's truck. Each thought he deserved it."

"Who got it?"

"Nobody. It rusted in a yard." She smiled. "Then last spring Tomás's brother had a stroke. Small one. Tomás drove six hours without telling me. I found a note on the counter that said, *Going to see Rafael.* That's all. When he came back he was quiet for two days. Then he said, 'I was so busy being right that I forgot to have a brother.'"

Julian looked at the ceiling tiles. "And that was it? Fixed?"

"No. They're still awkward. They talk about football like strangers. But they talk." She stood and tucked the blanket at his feet. "He said the hardest part was the first sentence. After that it was only sentences."

When she left, the room felt larger and emptier. Julian thought of Lena, whom he had last spoken to in a lawyer's office four years ago, both of them polite as knives. He had been so certain then. Work had been the reason, always the reason, and he had hidden inside it until she stopped knocking.

At dawn he asked for a pen and wrote on the back of a menu card. Then he deleted it all and typed on his phone instead, thumbs clumsy:

*Lena, I'm in St. Mary's with pneumonia. I'm okay. I'm not writing for help. I'm writing because I'm sorry. I was absent in our marriage long before I left it. You deserved better than my excuses. That's all. I hope you're well.*

He sent it before he could improve it.

She came the next afternoon, coat still on, holding a paper bag. She looked older, and kinder somehow. "You always did get sick dramatically," she said, and he laughed until he coughed.

She brought soup, then a crossword, then, on the third day, her old stories about the neighbor's dog. The air between them thawed slowly, the way a window clears from the edges inward.

On discharge morning, Marisol's shift had ended; he left her a note of thanks at the desk, inadequate but sincere. Lena waited by the elevator with his bag over her shoulder.

"Julian," she said as the doors opened. "There's a place on Fulton. Good bread. Nothing fancy." She studied the floor numbers. "Dinner Friday, if you're up to it."

He felt the first sentence, the hardest one, already behind him.

"I'd like that," he said. "I'd like that very much."

"I don't have anyone," he said. "Leave it blank."

She took the clipboard, looked at the empty line, and handed it back. "Everybody has someone, Mr. Hart. Sometimes it's just someone they owe a phone call."

He gave her a dry laugh. But on the bus home, the remark sat in his chest like a stone, and by the time he reached his door, it had a name: Lena.

Six years since she'd left, and he had never said the thing he knew was true. That he'd worked himself into a wall, that he'd been absent even when he was in the room. He wrote her a letter instead of calling, because cowardice is patient. *I'm sorry. You were right about nearly everything. I hope you're well.* He mailed it before he could revise it into something smaller.

No reply came. Strangely, he found he could bear that. The apology had been the point.

Dolores had said *someone,* and he began to take the word literally. He knocked on the door across the hall and met Mrs. Abara, who sent him home with jollof rice and opinions about his thin curtains. He learned the upstairs couple, Theo and Priya, had a baby who refused to sleep, and he began taking the stairs two at a time to hand over earplugs and a casserole. Someone invited him to a Sunday barbecue in the courtyard. He went. He stayed three hours.

Then there was Martin.

His brother lived two states away and hadn't spoken to him since their mother's funeral, where they'd argued over the house, the money, and everything beneath them. Julian drafted forty versions of a message and sent the shortest: *I miss you. I was a jerk. Can I call?*

The phone rang within a minute. Martin's voice was rough. "You sound old," he said, and Julian laughed until his eyes stung. They talked for two hours. Martin drove up the following month, and they sat on Julian's small balcony, saying little and meaning all of it.

"Julian? It's Lena. I got your letter. Sorry it took so long. I needed to figure out what I wanted to say." A pause, wind in the receiver. "Would you like to walk by the river sometime? Saturday, maybe?"

He looked up at the pale, unfolding sky.

"I'd like that," he said. "I'd like that very much."

## story 3

The truck died at sundown with a cough and a long metallic sigh, and the desert swallowed the silence that followed.

"Alternator," said Teodor, slamming the hood. "Or the universe hates me."

"The universe is indifferent," said Calloway, the geologist, already crouched over a rock with her hand lens. "It's one of its better qualities."

Marguerite rolled her eyes and handed out water. Pip took his bottle without a word. He was twelve, and he hadn't spoken since the funeral six weeks ago. He sat on the tailgate with his knees drawn up, watching the horizon, where a bruise-colored cloud was building.

They decided to wait for morning. They never got the chance.

The storm hit with no warning but a wall of wind. Sand scoured the air until the world was a roaring brown blur. Teodor shouted something about staying by the truck, but the gust that followed knocked Marguerite sideways, and when she grabbed for Pip's sleeve, Calloway's arm was there, and then Calloway was gone, and Teodor's voice was coming from everywhere at once.

Marguerite crawled until she struck a boulder and wedged herself against it. Someone was already there. Small, shaking, clutching her wrist.

"Pip."

The wind took her voice, but his grip answered.

Hours seemed to pass. When the gale thinned to a hiss, stars appeared, hard and countless. Two flashlight beams wobbled toward them: Teodor and Calloway, scraped and furious with relief.

"The truck's buried," Teodor said. "Everything's gone. I can't tell which way the road is."

"North, I think," Calloway said, "but I lost my phone and my bearings."

Pip stood up. He dug into his jacket and pulled out a brass compass, dented at the rim, its glass scratched with age. Marguerite recognized it. His father had carried it on every hunting trip, every drive, every walk to the mailbox, as a joke.

Pip held it flat on his palm. The needle shivered, then settled. He pointed, northeast, and began to walk.

Nobody argued.

They followed him through the cold hours, over dunes and dry washes, Pip stopping every few hundred steps to check the needle, adjust, and continue. Calloway, who had a theory about everything, said nothing. Teodor limped and kept his complaints to himself. Marguerite walked close behind the boy, watching the small shoulders carry a weight that wasn't the compass.

The sky paled. Pink licked the edges of the mountains. Then, ahead, a thin gray line resolved into asphalt, and a truck far off flashed in the early light like a coin.

"The highway," Teodor breathed. "Kid, you did it."

Pip stopped at the shoulder. He looked down at the compass, and his face crumpled.

"He taught me," he said, his voice cracked and rusty. "He said if you're lost, you just have to trust the needle. But he didn't trust anything. He got lost anyway. He left."

Marguerite knelt in the gravel and pulled him in. He sobbed against her shoulder, great shuddering breaths, while Teodor and Calloway looked away, giving him the privacy of the vast, brightening land.

"He didn't leave you," she murmured. "Look where you brought us."

Pip closed his fingers around the brass, and the sun came up.

## story 4

The morning after the funeral, Mara drove to Cutter's Ridge because she could not stand the casseroles.

Her mother had walked this trail every Sunday for thirty years. Mara had come with her exactly twice, both times as a teenager, both times sulking. Now the switchbacks felt longer, the air sharp with pine and wet stone. She climbed until her lungs burned, until she reached the flat granite bench at the overlook where her mother always rested.

Under the bench, wedged into a gap between rock and wood, sat a plastic bag knotted against the weather. Inside was a sketchbook, its cover warped from damp.

Mara almost didn't open it. Her mother had never shown anyone her drawings. But the first page held two girls on a porch step, one tall and scowling, one small and holding a jar of fireflies. Mara knew the porch. She knew the jar.

She turned the pages slowly. The girls grew older as the pages went on. In one drawing, they stood at opposite ends of a long table, a turkey between them like a border. In another, the tall one stood on a doorstep holding a casserole dish, her face uncertain, while the smaller one half opened the door. Mara's throat tightened. She had been that tall one, eleven years ago, the Thanksgiving she'd shown up after two years of silence and left within the hour.

Each drawing narrowed the distance. The sisters sat in the same car. They shared a blanket at a graveside, a different one, their father's. They laughed at something off the page, their shoulders touching. Her mother had dated each sketch in the corner, small and neat, eleven years of hoping rendered in pencil.

Mara didn't remember most of those moments being so warm. Maybe her mother had drawn them as she wished they had been. Maybe she had seen something Mara had been too proud to see.

The last drawing showed the two sisters on this very bench, facing the valley. Only the outline of the second figure was done, the tall one, Mara, her face left blank. Beneath it, in her mother's hand: *Not yet.*

Mara sat down on the granite. Her hands shook as she dug a pencil stub from the bag. She had not drawn since college, but her fingers remembered the slope of a jaw, the stubbornness of a brow. She filled in the face. She gave it no scowl. She gave it wet eyes and an open mouth, the look of someone about to say a hard thing.

When she finished, she wrote the date beneath her mother's words and crossed out nothing.

Then she took out her phone. Ellen answered on the fourth ring, her voice careful. "Mara?"

"I'm sorry," Mara said. The words came out cracked and ugly and true. "I'm sorry I left. I'm sorry for all of it. I was wrong, and I was proud, and I wasted so much time."

Silence, then a breath that shook. "Where are you?"

"Mom's overlook. On the bench."

"Don't move," Ellen said.

Mara closed the sketchbook and held it on her lap. Below her, the valley brightened under the climbing sun. She heard nothing yet but wind and her own heartbeat, and she waited, and for the first time in years the waiting felt like hope.

## story 5

Okafor kept his ledger the way other men kept faith: daily, without exception, in blue ink. Thirty years in Onitsha's Main Market had taught him that memory lies and numbers do not. Every transistor, every capacitor, every sale was written down in his square, upright hand.

Except one name. Eleven years ago, he had written *Emeka — loan guarantee — ₦4,200,000*, and a year later he had drawn a line through it so hard the pen tore the page.

The solar-fan venture had been Emeka's dream: small panels, quiet motors, cheap enough for any household in a country where light flickered like a bad promise. Okafor had cosigned because Emeka was his younger brother, because their mother had asked him to look after the boy, because the drawings looked sound. Then the supplier vanished, the fans arrived cracked, and the bank came for collateral. They took Okafor's stock, shelf by shelf, while he stood with his arms folded so no one would see them shake.

Emeka went north to Kano. Okafor did not ask where. Neither wrote. At their mother's funeral they stood on opposite sides of the grave, and the silence between them was the loudest thing there.

Okafor rebuilt slowly, with smaller orders and tighter margins. He trusted no one with his money, not even himself.

Then came Chidinma, his daughter, home from university with a clipboard and a stubborn jaw. "Papa, I surveyed two hundred households in Nkwo and Fegge. Hundred and forty-one said they would buy a solar fan under ten thousand naira. Hundred and forty-one."

"People say many things when they are not paying."

"I asked who had already bought a cheap one that broke. Ninety. They want a good one." She set the pages in front of him, tidy tables and neat percentages. He recognized the habit and disliked how proud it made him. "Uncle Emeka's design was right, Papa. Only the supplier was wrong."

He read the pages three times that night. In the morning he bought a bus ticket to Kano.

The journey took fourteen hours. Emeka's workshop sat behind a tyre shop, a tin-roofed room humming with a soldering iron. He was grayer, thinner, and he stood when Okafor appeared in the doorway, wiping his hands again and again on a rag.

"Brother," Emeka said.

"I came about fans," said Okafor, because it was the only way he could come at all. Then his voice broke. "I came because I was a stubborn man for eleven years."

Emeka laughed wetly. "Two of us, then."

They talked until the generator died. Emeka had kept building, one prototype at a time, testing them on neighbors. Okafor laid out his terms the way he laid out everything: a small first batch of fifty, a vetted supplier whom he would inspect personally, payments logged on both sides, no loans, no guarantees. "Careful," he said. "Slow."

"Careful," Emeka agreed. "I have learned the word."

Back in Onitsha, Okafor opened the ledger to a clean page. He uncapped the blue pen and wrote, in his square, upright hand: *Emeka Okafor — partner — 50 units.*

He looked at the name a long while. Then he turned back to the torn page, and beside the crossed-out line he wrote, smaller: *Settled.*

The bell above the door still had the same cracked ring. Okafor remembered it from childhood, when his father used to bring him here on Saturdays to buy spare parts for radios no one else could fix.

Nwosu looked up from behind the counter, and his smile faltered when he saw the folder in Okafor's hand.

"Chinedu," he said. "It has been a long time. You are an engineer now, I heard."

"Eleven years since the fire." Okafor laid the folder on the glass counter and opened it. "The fire service report. It took me three years to get a copy. Page four." He turned it around. "The capacitors in my father's workshop were counterfeit. Relabeled, underrated. They failed, and the unit burned. The batch number matches invoices from this shop."

Nwosu did not look at the page. He looked at his hands, spotted now and thinner than Okafor remembered. Outside, a generator coughed to life, and the fluorescent tube above them flickered.

"I knew you would come one day," Nwosu said. "I have rehearsed what to say. All of it is gone now."

"Then say the truth."

The old man sat down heavily on his stool. "I bought that lot from a trader at Alaba. It was cheap, and times were hard. I told myself the labels were honest. I did not test them, because testing costs money." He swallowed. "Two weeks later your father came in, and I sold him six. When I heard about the fire, I knew. I sat in this shop and I knew."

Okafor had prepared for denial. He had prepared shouting. He had not prepared for how small the man looked, or for the silence that settled after the confession, heavy as dust.

"You never said anything," he said at last. "Not at the funeral. Not once."

"I was a coward." Nwosu raised his eyes. "But I did one thing. Your school fees, after your father died. The envelope the church said came from an anonymous well-wisher, every term until you finished university." He gave a thin, broken laugh. "It was not kindness, Chinedu. Do not mistake it. I was trying to buy back something that cannot be bought."

Okafor felt the floor tilt. He thought of the nights he had thanked God for that envelope, and of the prayers he had said for a stranger.

He closed the folder slowly. "I came here to destroy you. I was going to take this to the police, the market association, everyone."

"You still can. I will not stop you."

"I know." Okafor looked along the shelves: the bins of diodes, the tangled coils of wire, the dim shop his father had loved. "But my firm needs someone to test incoming components. Someone who knows every trick counterfeiters use, because he has used the cheap ones himself. The pay is honest and the work is hard. If you ever pass a bad part, I will know, and I will finish what I started today."

Nwosu stared at him. "You are offering me work?"

"I am not offering forgiveness. I do not have that yet. Maybe I never will." Okafor's voice caught. "This is only a beginning."

The old man's shoulders shook once. He wiped his face with the back of his hand, then reached beneath the counter and pulled out a cardboard box overflowing with loose resistors, their colored bands bright as candy.

"These are mixed," he said quietly. "Someone should sort them."

Okafor hesitated, then pulled up the second stool. Side by side, without speaking, they began to read the bands and sort them into the empty bins.

## story 7

The ramp still smelled of fresh lumber and sealant when Marta put her hand on the rail. It was a good rail, smooth and sturdy, painted the same gray as the old library steps beside it. Eighty-one years had taught her to distrust anything that looked too new, but she trusted this.

"Take your time, Grandma," Lucy said, hovering at her elbow.

"I have taken my time for eighty-one years. I'm not about to rush now."

Lucy laughed, and the sound loosened something in Marta's chest. She started up, her cane tapping the boards, the slope so gentle that she hardly needed to lean on it. At the top, the double doors of the community hall stood propped open, and a volunteer in a paper badge smiled out at her as though this were nothing remarkable.

But it was remarkable. Marta stopped halfway and let herself look at the sidewalk below.

That was where they had put Dolores.

It was 1988, a cold November, and Dolores had been in her wheelchair for six years by then, since the accident at the cannery. She had never missed an election. She'd wheeled up to this same hall in her good wool coat, lipstick on, and found the steps waiting like a row of teeth. Marta had been beside her, younger and furious.

"We'll go in the back," Marta had said.

"The back is locked," Dolores answered.

In the end a poll worker had carried a clipboard and a ballot out to the curb. Dolores filled it out on her lap while people walked past, glancing, slowing down. A man read his newspaper and pretended not to watch. Someone's child asked loudly why that lady was voting outside. Dolores kept her chin high and her pen steady, but her ears had gone red, and Marta saw her hand tremble when she folded the paper. A worker took it from her, held it up, and said, "Got one out here," as if announcing a delivery.

Afterward Dolores said only, "Well. It counts the same." She never spoke of it again, and she never voted again either. She died eleven years later, and Marta had carried the humiliation for both of them ever since, like a stone in a coat pocket.

"Grandma?" Lucy said softly. "You okay?"

"Fine." Marta wiped her cheek with a knuckle. "Just thinking about a friend."

Lucy had collected four hundred signatures last spring, going door to door with a clipboard and a stubbornness Marta recognized. She had stood up at the council meeting with her voice shaking and spoken about her grandmother's knees, and about everyone else's too. Marta had not known, until the ramp was funded, that Lucy had also been speaking about Dolores.

At the top, Marta went inside. The booth had a curtain, and she drew it closed behind her. The silence was soft and private. Nobody watched her hands. Nobody read over her shoulder.

She marked her ballot slowly, carefully, and whispered, "This one's yours, Dolores."

Something in her chest, held tight for thirty-six years, let go all at once. She stood there breathing, tears falling freely, no one to see.

When she slid the ballot into the machine, it counted the same. But this time, it felt like more.

## story 8

The Crooked Ramp

Lucy built the ramp in July out of scrap lumber, a borrowed level that turned out to be wrong, and stubbornness. It leaned left like a tired man. Three steps led up to her grandmother's back door, and for six years Marta had not gone down them.

"It's crooked," Lucy admitted, standing at the bottom with a hammer.

Marta sat in the doorway in her wheelchair, gripping the armrests. "So is the whole house. Push me."

"Grandma, wait, I should—"

"Push."

The chair rolled, wobbled, drifted toward the edge, and Lucy ran alongside with a hand on the frame, steering it back. Then they were in the yard, in the grass, under a sky Marta hadn't stood beneath since her hip gave out. She tipped her head back and laughed until she coughed.

After that she wanted everything. The mailbox. The corner. The Thursday farmers' market, where Lucy pushed her along the ramp's cousin, the curb cut, while neighbors said, "Marta! We thought you'd moved."

It was at the market, beside a table of peaches, that Marta asked for the microphone the church ladies used for announcing raffle numbers. Lucy thought she wanted to thank everyone.

"In 1987," Marta said, and the speaker squealed, "four hundred dollars went missing from the Fairmount Grocery register. I took it. I was twenty-nine, behind on rent, and scared. They blamed Edith Pruitt, who stocked shelves there. She lost her job and never got another good one. I let her. I've known for thirty-some years, and I said nothing."

The market went quiet. A man set down his peach.

"Edith died in 2009. I can't tell her I'm sorry. So I'm telling you."

Lucy stood frozen, hands on the chair's handles, feeling her grandmother's shoulders shake. She had never known. Nobody had.

Within a month, Marta had sold her late husband's land out past the county road. She used the money to buy a wheelchair-accessible van. Painted on the side, in blue letters Lucy helped choose, were the words: PRUITT RIDES — FREE TRANSPORT FOR NEIGHBORS. Volunteers drove it to clinics, groceries, and church. Edith's niece came from Dayton for the dedication and held Marta's hands without speaking for a long time.

The county inspector arrived in September after a complaint about unpermitted construction. He was a heavyset man named Mr. Alvarado with a clipboard and a face prepared for conflict. He looked at the ramp, then at the van parked beside it, then at the old woman in the doorway who said, "Go ahead, write it up."

He didn't write anything for a minute. "My mother," he finally said, "didn't leave her apartment her last four years. Third floor. No ramp would've helped."

He went to his truck and came back with a real level and a drill.

Lucy held boards while he re-cut the supports, explaining slope ratios, one inch of rise for every twelve of run. "Your instinct was good," he said. "Just needed math."

On Saturday, the ramp stood straight for the first time. Marta rolled down it slowly, testing, and stopped at the bottom.

"Well?" Lucy asked.

"Boring," Marta said, smiling. "Perfectly boring. I love it."

Then she rolled on toward the van, where someone was waiting for a ride.

Nora Castellan had been hunched over the ledgers for eleven hours when the cleaning crew rolled their cart onto the twenty-eighth floor. She waved them off without looking up.

"Give me another hour," she said. They never pushed back when she was like this, and tonight was no different.

The Ridgemont Corporation's records covered seven years and nearly forty thousand transactions. The junior associates had finished their review two weeks earlier and found nothing. The partners wanted the case closed. A settlement offer sat at the corner of her desk, a tidy sum that would make the entire problem disappear.

Still, something gnawed at her. It was too clean.

"We're doing it again," she had told a conference room full of groans that morning. "Every entry. Every invoice. Every vendor."

"Nora, we already did it," said Peter, the youngest, gray with exhaustion. "Everything reconciles."

"Of course it reconciles," she said. "Anyone can make numbers balance. I want to know *why* they balance."

Now, close to midnight, she pulled another folder toward her and began again. Her glasses had crept down her nose, and her coffee had gone cold hours before. She traced a finger down a column of retainer payments to a firm called Clearwater Strategies. Nine thousand dollars. Nine thousand. Nine thousand.

She stopped.

Every payment was exactly nine thousand dollars, sent on the first of the month. Real consultants billed for real work, and real work was messy. It varied. It overran. It came with expense reports and strange small charges for airport sandwiches.

She paged back through the years. For five years Clearwater had been paid without missing a month or deviating by a single cent.

Nora grabbed her phone and called Peter. He picked up on the sixth ring, his voice thick with sleep.

"Pull the incorporation records for Clearwater Strategies," she said.

"Now? It's midnight."

"Now."

She listened to him scrabbling for his laptop. Twenty minutes later he called back, fully awake.

"Nora, it was formed five years ago. The registered agent's address is a rented apartment. And the owner of record..." He paused. "It's Thomas Ridgemont's brother-in-law."

"Nine thousand a month for five years," she said. "Five hundred forty thousand dollars in plain sight, and nobody questioned it because it looked so orderly."

"The first review missed it," Peter said quietly.

"The first review was looking for chaos. Fraud doesn't always look like chaos. Sometimes it looks like discipline." She lifted the settlement offer and fed it into the shredder beside her chair. The machine whined and swallowed it whole. "Go back to sleep. Tomorrow we find out what else is orderly."

When the call ended, she stood and stretched, her back cracking. Outside the window, the city glittered, vast and indifferent. Somewhere among those lights, people were trusting the numbers to be honest.

She sat back down and opened the next folder.

## story 10

Nadia Rahman had been bent over the ledgers for eleven hours when the night cleaning crew rolled their cart onto the thirty-first floor. She waved them off without looking up.

Nadia seized her phone and called Felix, who answered on the ninth ring, groggy.

"Now? It's midnight."

"Now."

"Nadia, it was incorporated five years ago. The registered agent is a mail-drop shop. And the listed owner..." He hesitated. "It's Martin Hargrove's brother-in-law."

She sat down again and opened the next binder.

## story 11

Marisol released her folded ballot, and it drifted to the bottom of the box with a soft, papery whisper, like a sleeper shifting in bed. Her hands were empty now, but she kept listening.

Folding tables lined the walls, each manned by a volunteer with a name tag and the same weary, gentle smile. The ballot box sat in the center of the floor like an altar, gray steel, dented at one corner where someone long ago had struck it in anger.

"Glad you came, ma'am," said the young man by the entrance. He looked barely old enough to vote himself. His badge read ASK ME ABOUT THE NEW SYSTEM.

"Thank you for being here," Marisol said, answering his eagerness with a smile.

The question before the town was a plain one. Should paper ballots give way to a row of glowing touchscreens, silent and swift, that could tally votes in minutes? Supporters called it progress. Opponents called it the loss of something they couldn't quite name.

Marisol hadn't known her own mind until she stood in the booth, pencil raised. She had thought of her late husband, Anselmo, who had worked this precinct for thirty years, counting by hand beside a mug of scorched coffee. He used to say a paper ballot was a promise you could hold. "A screen only claims it kept its word," he'd say. "Paper lets you check."

Then she had thought of her granddaughter Lucía, who two elections ago had waited in this very hall, in her wheelchair, for forty minutes because the booth was too narrow to enter. Lucía had said nothing on the drive home, and the quiet had been worse than any complaint.

So Marisol had marked her choice, folded the paper, and let go.

Now she lingered near the door, watching the others. A man in a work jacket jammed his ballot in without a glance. A girl voting for the first time held hers a moment too long, as if saying farewell. Each of them met the metal in their own way, a small knock for a small decision.

That was it, Marisol realized. The sound was the argument itself. Old and new, caution and hope, all tapping together in a plain room that smelled of floor wax and stale coffee.

"Did you vote for the new system?" the young man asked, unable to help himself.

"That's private," she said.

"Sorry. I just hope it passes. It'd be quicker. Easier on people."

"Easier matters," Marisol said. "So does trusting what you can't see."

He nodded slowly, as though she had set something heavier in his hands than he'd expected.

Outside, the afternoon was bright and ordinary. On the steps Marisol turned and looked back through the glass doors at the box, still taking ballots, still knocking. Whatever the count decided tonight, someone would stand in that hall next year and argue again, because that was what the room was for.

She thought Anselmo would have liked that part.

She walked to her car, and behind her, faintly, another ballot fell.

Odalys Crane had drunk fog on four continents, and every one of them had tried to make her quit.

"Miss Crane," said the pier warden, a gaunt man named Pellam who plainly hadn't slept. "This jetty is condemned. The north arm comes down at first light."

"Then it may come down with me aboard."

"Please. Be reasonable."

"I'm known," she said into the planks, "for the opposite."

It was the oldest in the port, an iron brute the size of a cart, dropped in 1889 to moor a steamer called the *Verity*, which had rusted to pieces decades ago. The anchor had remained. Divers had tried to lift it. Cables had parted. Last autumn a trawler had hauled until its engine shrieked, and the anchor had not shifted an inch. It belonged to the seabed now, a relic of the harbor's long quarrel with the world.

The wreckers wanted the jetty gone, and the anchor's chain ran beneath it. Neither could be cleared without the other.

Pellam eased himself down beside her with a groan. Fog was sliding in, thick and slow, erasing the posts one at a time.

"Why do you do it?" he asked. "The fog. Everyone wonders."

Odalys was quiet for a while. Then she parted her lips, let a pale thread curl in, held it, and sent it out again.

"When I was small, my father took a skiff out in weather like this," she said. "The fog closed over him and he never returned. People said it took him. So I resolved that since it had taken from me, I would take from it. A little at a time, all my life."

"That isn't stubbornness," Pellam said softly. "That's grief."

"Same beast, different coat."

"The anchor won't move because it won't," he said. "Nor will you. But the jetty needn't fall on either of you. If we changed the plan and kept the last stretch, the part over the chain..."

"A memorial," Odalys said.

"A stubborn one."

She weighed it with a judge's solemnity. The fog wound around them, cold and patient, and for once she did not drink it.

"Very well," she said at last, freeing one hand. "But I stay until the papers are signed."

"Naturally."

"And I'm having whatever fog comes tonight."

Pellam smiled and drew his coat tighter. "I'd expect nothing less."

Beneath them the anchor held, and above it, so did she.
