# E6 pilot: do visible human attributes survive the loop, and what replaces them?

**Both arms complete and clean.** `e6_photo` and `e6_track`: 192 trajectories × 20 steps each (36 occupation seeds and 12 portrait seeds, 4 generator seeds per prompt), SDXL + llava:latest, one start each, no restart. Pod time 3.4 h and 3.6 h at 3.2–3.3 s/trajectory-step. Coded by the validated judge (Claude Sonnet 5.5, `reports/e6_check_set/README.md`; kappa vs Brad 0.83 / 0.76 / 0.71 on gender / age / skin) on all 7,680 images, Haiku 5.5 as second opinion, YuNet + CelebAMask facial L* on every image; one-shot SDXL baseline (450 images) judged the same way.

**Headline.** The loop drops the seeded attributes within a handful of steps, and what replaces them is the *loop's* prior, not the occupation's one-shot prior: faces go light-skinned in one to two steps whatever the seed and whatever the occupation (dark seeds' mean facial L* 25 → 50 at step 1, ≈ 53 for every seed band from step 2), women turn into men in every occupation except nurse, single people turn into crowds and collages. Telling the captioner to state age, gender and skin tone (Tracking) roughly doubles how long gender and age last, does nothing for skin tone, and creates its own attractor: captions that say "individuals of various ages, genders and skin tones" and images that are grids of dozens of faces (55% of Tracking images by step 19).

## What is lost, and how fast

Survival is relative to what the judge saw at step 0 (SDXL renders "light-skinned" as medium in 32 of 63 seeds and "medium-skinned" as dark in 19 of 64; gender and age realised in 99–100%). Person dropout is a competing risk. n = 144 occupation and 48 portrait trajectories per arm.

| gender, Photo, occupation / portrait | 0.47 / 0.83 | 0.23 / 0.63 | 0.09 / 0.25 | 0.80 / 0.73 | 0.10 / 0.02 |
| gender, Tracking | 0.59 / 0.83 | 0.34 / 0.54 | 0.13 / 0.31 | 0.83 / 0.69 | 0.05 / 0.00 |
| age, Photo | 0.23 / 0.54 | 0.08 / 0.21 | 0.01 / 0.06 | 0.90 / 0.90 | 0.09 / 0.04 |

One step is enough. Every dark-skinned seed (64 trajectories per arm) has left the dark band by step 5 in both arms, and the hazard does not depend on the occupation: dark pilots (whose one-shot prior is light) 0.70 per step, dark social workers (whose one-shot prior is dark) 0.82 in Photo; 0.52 vs 0.58 in Tracking. Light seeds keep their band (hazard 0.02–0.11). The whole "asymmetry" in prediction 3 is this: skin is lost in one direction, toward light, for everyone. Within-trajectory, only 19–39% of faces are still within 10 L* of their own step 0 by step 19.

Gender changes by occupation (losses from a kept state, Photo; Tracking in brackets):

| occupation | woman → man | man → woman | → ambiguous |
|---|---|---|---|
| pilot | 24 (33) | 7 (16) | 12 (12) |

Women become men in every group except nurse, where the two directions balance; the social worker, whose one-shot prior is 97% women, loses women to men three to one. Age losses run both ways within each occupation (Photo, young → older vs older → young: nurse 61 vs 58, pilot 26 vs 20, social worker 68 vs 44, portrait 56 vs 58) and read as judge boundary noise plus the drift to crowds, not as a directional effect. Pre-registered definition: a judge call of "ambiguous" counts as a loss; with it counted as unknown instead, gender kept at step 10 rises to 31% / 77% (Photo, occupation / portrait) and the counter-vs-stereotypical hazard ratio stays at 1.1.

## What replaces the attributes

**The occupation leaves the caption before the attribute does.** Share of captions naming the job or its setting (pilot / cockpit / aircraft; nurse / hospital / scrubs / patient; social worker / office / desk / client) at steps 0, 5, 10, 18: pilot 1.00, 0.88, 0.83, 0.65; nurse 0.98, 0.81, 0.69, 0.50; social worker 0.96, 0.81, 0.77, 0.60 (Photo; Tracking lower: 0.25–0.42 at step 18). The job title itself: "pilot" in 85% of step-0 captions, "nurse" in 48%, **"social worker" in 0%** at every step of both arms. LLaVA never recognises a social worker; from step 0 that seed is "a person at a desk", so the social worker's one-shot prior (50% dark-skinned, 97% women) has no channel into the loop. That is why the images converge on the generic prior instead: the one-shot "a photograph of a person" set is 56% men, 54% light / 42% medium skin, L* 55.5 (`reports/e6/oneshot_prior.json`; pilot one-shot 89% men, 86% light, facial L* 39.3; nurse 99% women, 81% light, L* 41.3; social worker 97% women, 50% dark / 38% medium, L* 42.1). The loop's step-19 faces (L* 52–58) are lighter than every occupation's own one-shot faces and match the neutral set.

**The captioner stops naming the attributes.** Share of captions naming a value (keyword lists in `analysis/e6_report.py`) at steps 0, 1, 2, 5, 10, 18:

| | gender | age | skin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Photo | 0.81, 0.82, 0.71, 0.54, 0.38, 0.29 | 0.47, 0.33, 0.22, 0.15, 0.12, 0.12 | 0.14, 0.08, 0.06, 0.04, 0.01, 0.02 |
| Tracking | 0.92, 0.91, 0.91, 0.82, 0.73, 0.67 | 0.80, 0.72, 0.68, 0.57, 0.51, 0.46 | 0.77, 0.69, 0.68, 0.45, 0.35, 0.28 |

Under Photo the loop is a chain of omissions: 87% of losses happen at an image whose caption named no value at all. Under Tracking LLaVA does state a value most of the time, yet skin survival barely moves (kept at step 5: 10% vs 5%), because (a) 52 of 113 counter-stereotypical skin losses follow a caption that names a *different* band, usually "skin tone is fair / light" for a face that was dark or medium, so the captioner lightens the person before the generator does; and (b) SDXL renders "light-skinned" and "medium-skinned" as the same overlapping distribution (check set: L* 47 ± 12 vs 42 ± 13), so even a correct caption cannot hold a medium band.

**The Tracking instruction creates a collage attractor.** "State the apparent age, gender and skin tone of any person" turns, once two or more people are in frame, into "individuals of various ages, genders and skin tones" (0% of Photo captions; Tracking 2% at step 2, 7% at step 5, 17% at step 18), which SDXL renders as grids of dozens of faces. Images with five or more people: Photo 24% at step 5, 38% at step 19; Tracking 35% and 55%. Single-person images fall from 78–98% at step 0 to 15–23% at step 19 in both arms. Tracking also keeps people in frame (91% vs 64% at step 19 for occupation seeds) and pushes toward black-and-white and sepia (38% of Tracking images at step 19 vs 16%): joint cluster 6 below, vintage group photographs of uniformed men, is 91% Tracking.

## Occupation re-assignment: nurse → doctor, pilot → soldier / astronaut (Brad's question, 2026-10-23; `analysis/e6_occupations.py`, `reports/e6/occupations.md`)

The captions do re-assign the job, early and often. Of the 48 nurse trajectories per arm, 39 (Photo) / 37 (Tracking) call someone a doctor, physician or surgeon at some step (median first step 3); 39 / 39 call someone a scientist or lab worker; 39 / 33 a patient. Of the 48 pilot trajectories, 42 / 44 become military (soldier, army, air force, camouflage; median first step 2, and under Tracking the military share stays at 54% of captions at step 19 while "pilot" falls to 12%); 18 / 6 become astronauts. Social workers become nothing in particular: no job word ever appears for them except "office".

**The doctor label follows the gender in the image, not the seed.** Whether a nurse trajectory ever gets "doctor" does not depend on its seed (men 83% / women 79% in Photo, 83% / 71% in Tracking; skin bands 62–94% with no consistent order). What decides the word is who is in the picture when LLaVA writes it. Single-person images of nurse seeds, steps 1–19, Tracking arm (captions there are gendered): the caption says "doctor" for 18 men and 4 women, "nurse" for 1 man and 13 women, against a balanced 87 / 87 for all single-person images (Fisher p < 0.001). Photo arm: "doctor" 12 men / 2 women, "nurse" 2 / 2 (p = 0.2, few nurse captions). The caption's own pronoun says the same: at the first "doctor" caption, "man" 12 + "man and woman" 12 vs "woman" 6 (Tracking). Skin: "doctor" images are 20 of 22 light, "nurse" images 9 light / 2 medium / 4 dark, baseline 147 / 14 / 13. So the sequence is: the generator turns the nurse into a man (and light-skinned) by drift, then the captioner re-labels the man as a doctor and keeps "nurse" for the women. A male nurse seed is called a doctor at a median of step 2 while the image still shows a man (14 of 20 first "doctor" captions in Photo, 16 of 20 in Tracking); a female nurse seed is called a doctor at a median of step 4, by which time the image shows a man in 7 of 19 (Photo) / 5 of 17 (Tracking) cases.

**Pilot → military is not gendered or coloured; pilot → astronaut is male.** Single-person images of pilot seeds: "military" captions describe 73 men / 88 women (Tracking) and 28 / 32 (Photo), the same split as "pilot" captions (54 / 75; 36 / 45) and as all single-person pilot images (117 / 107; 81 / 67). Skin bands under "military" (127 light / 26 medium / 11 dark) match the baseline (182 / 30 / 15). SDXL's female pilot and female soldier are both common renders, so this conversion carries the seed's gender through. The astronaut conversion is different: "astronaut" captions describe 35 men / 11 women in Photo and 22 / 0 in Tracking (vs "military", Fisher p = 0.003 and < 0.001), and in Photo 11 of the 18 astronaut trajectories started from woman seeds. A woman pilot in the loop becomes a man in a spacesuit.

## Judge reliability on the full set

Sonnet vs Haiku on all images where both gave a value (kappa): gender 0.84–0.89 by seed group, age 0.63–0.86, skin 0.73–0.81, with no seed group (skin light / medium / dark, man / woman, occupation / portrait) systematically worse, so the per-group error rate does not drive the group differences above. Sonnet parsed 7,669 of 7,680 on the first pass; 10 replies that contained a corrected second JSON object were salvaged by `analysis/judge_repair.py` (last complete object), 1 is null; Haiku hit max_tokens on 22 (null). Judge calls: about 16,200 (Sonnet 8,130 including the one-shot set, Haiku the rest), 12.0 M input and 7.3 M output tokens; about $30 for Sonnet at the check-set rate.

## Deviations and caveats

- Skin "kept" by judge band is harsh at the light/medium boundary (judge–Brad kappa 0.71, bands overlap in SDXL renders); the L* table is the robust readout and tells the same story. Step-0 judge bands over the 192 seeds are 30 light / 72 medium / 89 dark against 64 / 64 / 64 seeded.
- The caption keyword check had two bugs found on this data and fixed before any number above was read: punctuation after a keyword ("dark skin,") was not matched, and the judge's occasional literal string "null" counted as a changed value. Skin phrasings LLaVA actually uses under Tracking ("skin tone is fair", "darker skin tone") were added to the matcher after inspecting the captions; the mechanism split (omission / override / caption change) depends on this list and is reported as such.
- Judge "ambiguous" gender is a loss by the pre-registered definition; the sensitivity with it as unknown is given above and changes no verdict.
- The memory-curve table (`reports/memory_curve.md`) lists E6 with half-life 1 at the trajectory level; that is the four same-prompt replicas, see the seed-level figures above.
- `analysis/e6_report.py` gained the joint survival, stereotypicality hazards with Wilson intervals, framing and end-of-horizon columns after the first build; nothing pre-registered was removed.
- Figures: `reports/e6/strips_{pilot,nurse,social_worker,portrait}.jpg` (first ten steps of one replica per seed, Photo row above Tracking row), `reports/e6/e6_report.md` (all tables), `reports/e6_vs_a0_photo/*`.

## What this means for the design

1. **The one-shot prior is the wrong reference for the loop.** The quantity that matters is the loop's own stationary prior for "a person" (light, male, then no single person at all), which the occupation only modulates while the captioner still names it. A follow-up should seed occupations the captioner can see (pilot, nurse, chef, police officer; not social worker) and measure the occupation's own survival alongside the attributes.
2. **Skin tone is the fragile attribute and the captioner is half the problem.** An instruction that makes LLaVA name the skin tone does not help because LLaVA names it wrong, and SDXL cannot render a medium band on request. A stronger captioner (the E6 Claude cell in SPEC2 §11) is the obvious next arm; A0-S showed Sonnet drops people faster, so the result is not predictable.
3. **Measurement instructions change the dynamics.** Tracking is not a passive probe: it created the collage attractor. Any "ask the captioner to track X" design needs an attractor check against its own control.
